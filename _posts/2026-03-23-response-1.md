## Visual 1: Heatmap of Names with Google’s Gemini

![Heat map of character names]({{ '/images/Fig1-HeatMap-CharacterNames.jpeg' | relative_url }})

*Figure 1: Frequency of character names across texts.*

I shared this visualization with Google’s Gemini AI model without context. I also renamed the file to just “Fig1” to avoid giving context from the file’s name. 

![Screenshot1 of Gemini Chat]({{ '/images/fig1-ss1.png' | relative_url }})

The LLM immediately interpreted the visualization as an analysis of character name mentions in both canon and fanfiction texts. It even went on to analyze the numbers, including how Harry dominated all texts despite some being fanfiction texts, as well as how Ron and Hermione consistently take the #2 and #3 spots in the original texts.


![Screenshot2 of Gemini Chat]({{ '/images/fig1-ss2.png' | relative_url }})

Since the AI model got almost everything correct, I decided to ask if it was just making assumptions. It replied and started explaining how it should have looked at the numbers/facts of what’s on screen rather than assuming anything first, mentioning things like the texts with most characters mentioned overall, and the texts where some characters did not appear at all.

The LLM later explained how it made the previous assumptions. It first mentioned how it ultimately made the assumption that I was comparing canon with fanfiction works based on the file names in the visualization. It criticized the way the computational text analysis worked, like how it treated them as different strings of text and not as characters themselves, Gemini was able to identify that “Severus” and “Snape” are in fact the same character (first and last name), but the computer analysis did not. 

Something interesting the AI model raised was that the character could be assumed to be absent from a certain text simply because their name wasn’t mentioned. However, the author could refer to the characters without using their name, like how Harry Potter is sometimes referred to as “The Boy Who Lived” in the original texts.


![Screenshot3 of Gemini Chat]({{ '/images/fig1-ss3.png' | relative_url }})

Here I asked if the data could be interpreted differently, and its first reply was expected. In my research, I did try finding fanfiction texts with similar word counts, but when it came to the canon texts, I relied more on how different the Harry Potter books would be at the start of the series and the end. All in all, there could have been more interesting results from my previous analysis by calculating the relative frequency of words.

Another limitation to my research was the mention of characters' last names and/or names they might be called often in the books being counted as one. As we previously talked about in the chat, Voyant Tools cannot count “Severus Snape” as just “Snape” or “Severus,” which is why I decided to put them separately. However, I found Gemini’s interpretation on “Formal Vs. Informal” words to be quite intriguing. By interpreting the visualization this way, I think I could have taken this approach to help support some points I had made in the section about “Institutional Vs Relational Vocabulary” in the first assignment.

## Visual 2: Institutional and Relational Vocabulary Bar Chart with Perplexity

![Institutional and relational vocabulary]({{ '/images/Fig2-Institutional&Relational-Vocab.jpeg' | relative_url }})

*Figure 2: Comparison of institutional vs relational terms.*

For this visualization, I used perplexity AI and again provided the image without additional context to find out how the model would interpret it at first.


![Screenshot1 of Perplexity Chat]({{ '/images/fig2-ss1.png' | relative_url }})

This figure does not show all the file names clearly, so I assumed the responses will differ between the AI models. However, I was a bit surprised because Perplexity took the safe route and did not initially assume anything about the kind of corpora that was provided, unlike Gemini who immediately assumed the files were Harry Potter and Harry Potter fanfiction texts.


![Screenshot2 of Perplexity Chat]({{ '/images/fig2-ss2.png' | relative_url }})

Perplexity started doing basic analysis, where it analyzes the highest frequencies of words and started making comparisons between the files with the name ‘Rowling’ in them. I can still see Perplexity being careful with the information it's providing, as it’s suggesting that the Rowling texts are Harry Potter models, but didn’t explicitly mention it. At this point, it hasn’t noticed the distinction between institutional words in Harry Potter and relational vocabulary; it also didn’t give me a clear explanation as to what the other texts are exactly.


![Screenshot3 of Perplexity Chat]({{ '/images/fig2-ss3.png' | relative_url }})

There we go, now it explicitly stated that the corpora is a combination of canon and fan-written Harry Potter texts. The AI model also caught on to the relational vocabulary here, where it said that fanfiction relies on words that suggests there are more relationships or romance in the stories. Perplexity was spot on with what I had intended in my research in this visualization, because it came to the conclusion that fanfiction texts tend to have fewer magical elements in them and mix canon language with a setting that each fanfiction writer intends for their story.


![Screenshot4 of Perplexity Chat]({{ '/images/fig2-ss4.png' | relative_url }})

Here, Perplexity acknowledges the limitations of distant reading as we can never be sure without reading the texts ourselves. Ted Underwood’s analysis on distant reading in his book, Distant Horizons, gives a similar warning of the dangers of distant reading where he mentions that although a computer shows patterns of word frequencies and distribution, it isn’t enough to draw reliable conclusions without carefully interpreting the data ourselves.
